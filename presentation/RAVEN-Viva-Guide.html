RAVEN — Viva Guide, page by page

For every slide: what you say · what the teacher can ask · how you answer in simple words. Extra depth on RAVEN-X, the metrics, and the live demo. All numbers are from our real runs.
3 golden rules for the whole viva
1. Speak in plain words first, show the number second. "It catches 90 out of 100 toxic comments" is better than "recall 0.90."
2. Never bluff. If you don't know, say "we did not measure that, but here is how we would." Honesty scores; a caught lie sinks the viva.
3. Bring every answer back to recall (catching toxic comments) and to RAVEN-X (our own new idea). Those are our strengths.
The whole project in 3 sentences (memorise this): "We built RAVEN, a system that reads a comment and says toxic or safe, and flags the toxic ones for a human moderator. On English we fine-tuned DistilBERT and it catches 90% of toxic comments. Then we built our own method, RAVEN-X, that also works on Hindi written in Devanagari — even pointing at which words are toxic — by transferring that skill from English, and it runs live in a website and a Chrome extension."
1Title — RAVEN · Hate-Speech Classification
What you say
"Good morning ma'am. Our project is RAVEN — a hate-speech classifier. It reads any social-media comment and decides if it is toxic or safe, so that human moderators only have to read the harmful ones. I will also show our own extension of it, RAVEN-X, for Hindi."
If the teacher asks
Why the name RAVEN?
It is just our project name for the system; the raven is a bird known for watching — our system watches comments.
What is hate speech, exactly?
Language that attacks or demeans a person or group — insults, threats, identity attacks. We separate it from normal rude talk, which we do NOT flag.
2Problem Statement
What you say
"Social media has too many comments for humans to read. Some speech incites violence or spreads misinformation. Missing one toxic comment is worse than wrongly flagging a clean one. So our goal is to automatically flag every comment that needs review, so a moderator reads only the few that matter, not the millions that do not."
If the teacher asks
Why can't a simple keyword/bad-word list do this?
Because the same word can be a joke or an attack — "you killed it" is praise. A word list has no context, so it both misses hidden hate and wrongly flags clean text.
Why is "missing a toxic comment" worse than a false alarm?
A false alarm just sends a clean comment to a human who clears it in a second. A missed toxic comment stays public and can cause real harm. That is why our main metric is recall — how many toxic ones we catch.
Who is the user?
A content-moderation team, or a normal user who wants toxic replies flagged as they scroll — which our Chrome extension does.
3Related Work
What you say
"We built on six well-known works. Davidson separated hate from merely rude language. BERT is the transformer our English model builds on. DistilBERT is a smaller, faster BERT — our live English classifier. MuRIL is a BERT for Indian languages — we use it to read Devanagari Hindi. Jigsaw is our English training data, and HASOC is our Hindi data."
If the teacher asks
What did YOU add on top of these?
Two things: (1) we fine-tuned DistilBERT into a working live product with a website and extension; (2) our own method RAVEN-X, which transfers word-level hate explanations from English to Hindi — that part is new, nobody in these papers did it.
Difference between BERT and DistilBERT?
DistilBERT is a compressed BERT — 40% smaller, 60% faster, keeps about 97% of the accuracy. HuggingFace made it by "distillation." We did not distil it ourselves; we fine-tuned the ready-made one.
What is MuRIL and why use it?
MuRIL is a BERT that Google pre-trained on 17 Indian languages plus English. So it already understands Devanagari Hindi, which our English DistilBERT cannot read.
4Datasets — Jigsaw (English) & HASOC (Hindi)
What you say
"Two datasets, two languages, very different sizes. Jigsaw — 1.8 million English Wikipedia comments, human-labelled, but only about 8% are toxic, so it is very imbalanced. HASOC-2019 — 4,665 Hindi posts in Devanagari, labelled hate/offensive or not. Hindi is tiny — about 380 times smaller — which is exactly the low-resource problem we solve."
If the teacher asks
Only 4,665 Hindi posts — isn't that too small?
Yes, and that IS the point. Hindi is a low-resource language; big labelled sets don't exist. If Hindi had millions of labels the project would be trivial. Our RAVEN-X borrows knowledge from English precisely because Hindi labels are few.
What does "imbalanced" mean and why does it matter?
Only 8% of comments are toxic, so a lazy model can call everything "safe" and still be 92% accurate — while catching zero toxic comments. That is why we judge by recall, not accuracy, and why we balanced the data when training.
How did you label / preprocess?
Jigsaw came human-labelled with a toxicity score; we made it binary (toxic if score ≥ 0.5). We lowercased text, removed URLs and @mentions, then tokenized. We used an 80/10/10 stratified train-validation-test split so the class balance is kept in every part.
What is a stratified split?
Splitting so each part (train/val/test) has the same toxic-to-safe ratio — so the test is a fair mirror of reality.
5Baselines — the accuracy trap
What you say
"We first tried simpler models. Look at the trap: TF-IDF and a single LSTM get 87–95% accuracy but their recall is only 0.45 — they miss more than half of toxic comments. Accuracy lied. Only the BiLSTM, reading context in both directions, reached 0.914 recall. This is why we moved to a transformer."

If the teacher asks
What is TF-IDF?
A simple method that counts how important each word is in a comment (rare words weigh more). It has no understanding of order or context — just word counts.
What is an LSTM, and why is BiLSTM better?
An LSTM is a network that reads a sentence word by word and remembers earlier words. BiLSTM reads it both forward and backward, so it understands a word using words on both sides — that context is why its recall jumped to 0.914.
BiLSTM already gets 0.914 recall — why not stop here?
Its accuracy dropped to 0.81 and it still misses subtle context and sarcasm. A transformer (DistilBERT) gives high recall AND stays accurate, and is small enough to serve live.
Whose work were the baselines?
The classical and RNN baselines were mainly a teammate's contribution; the transformer and the whole product/RAVEN-X side is our track. (Say this only if asked about division of work.)
6Why DistilBERT
What you say
"Problem: bag-of-words and single word-vectors have no context, so they miss sarcasm. Fix: a transformer reads the whole sentence with self-attention, so meaning comes from context, not keywords. Why the distilled one: full BERT is too big for our single GPU; DistilBERT is 40% smaller, 60% faster, keeps ~97% accuracy, small enough to run live. Effect: recall jumps from 0.45 to 0.90."
If the teacher asks
What is "self-attention" / a transformer, in simple words?
For each word, the model looks at every other word in the sentence and decides which ones matter for its meaning. So "kill" in "you killed it" is understood from "it" nearby, not treated as violence.
Did you distil BERT yourself?
No. DistilBERT was already distilled by HuggingFace. We took that ready model and fine-tuned it on our toxic-comment data. We never ran a teacher-student distillation ourselves.
What is fine-tuning?
Taking a model already trained on huge general text and training it a little more on our specific task (toxic vs safe). Cheap, and works with less data.
How big is the model?
66.9 million parameters, 6 transformer layers, 12 attention heads, hidden size 768. Input length capped at 128 tokens.
7DistilBERT results
What you say
"On 10,000 held-out toxic comments it missed only 1,011 — so recall 0.899. Accuracy 0.885, precision 0.875, F1 0.887, ROC-AUC 0.9515. The confusion matrix shows most errors are on the safe side, which is what we want — we would rather over-flag than miss toxic."
If the teacher asks — the METRICS (know these cold)
What is precision vs recall?
Precision = of the comments I called toxic, how many really were (avoids false alarms). Recall = of all truly toxic comments, how many I caught (avoids misses). We care most about recall.
What is F1?
The balance (harmonic mean) of precision and recall — one number that is high only when both are good.
What is a confusion matrix?
A 2×2 table: TN=8712 safe called safe, FP=1288 safe wrongly called toxic, FN=1011 toxic wrongly called safe (the misses), TP=8989 toxic caught. FN is the box we most want small.
What is ROC-AUC?
How well the model separates toxic from safe across all thresholds; 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is guessing. Ours is 0.9515 — strong separation.
Did the full 1.8 million go into DistilBERT?
The full dataset was used across the project; for the transformer fine-tune we used a balanced sample of 100,000 (50% toxic, 50% safe). On the raw 8% data the model just predicts "safe"; balancing forced it to learn the toxic class and lifted recall to 0.90.
How did you avoid overfitting?
We watched validation loss. Training for 3 epochs lowered training loss but raised validation loss — classic overfitting — so about 1 epoch was best. We report that honestly.
8Live software — web app + Chrome extension
What you say
"This is not just a notebook — it is a working product. The web app: paste any comment and get Toxic or Safe with a confidence score and the toxic words highlighted. The Chrome extension: it scores real posts inline on X as you scroll, right where the abuse happens. One model behind both, served by one API."

If the teacher asks
How does the extension work technically?
It reads the visible comments on the page, sends their text to our API, gets back toxic/safe + score, and draws a small coloured badge next to each. It caches results so scrolling stays fast.
What is the API / the stack?
A FastAPI service in Python. It loads the model once and answers a /predict request with the label and score. Website, extension and mobile all call the same API, so the language logic lives in one place.
Is it fast enough / real-time?
Yes — each comment is one quick forward pass; we cap input at 128 tokens and cache repeats, so results appear as you scroll.
9RAVEN-X — cross-script rationale transfer ★ (YOUR main part)
Say it simply first (the analogy)
"Imagine a teacher who taught a student to underline the rude words in English sentences. Then you give that student a Hindi sentence in Devanagari — and the student underlines the rude Hindi words too, even though nobody ever showed them rude Hindi words. That transfer is what RAVEN-X does. The 'X' means cross-script — English to Devanagari."
What you say (the real version)
Problem: we can label whether a Hindi comment is toxic, but we have no Hindi labels for which words are toxic — those word-level labels exist only in English. Idea: train one shared MuRIL model to do two jobs at once — decide toxic/not (Hindi + English) and highlight the toxic words (English only) — so the "point at the hateful word" skill transfers to Hindi. Why it can transfer: MuRIL puts an English slur and a Hindi slur in the same meaning space, even though the two scripts share zero letters. Result: macro-F1 0.842 vs 0.787 for TF-IDF — +5.5 points.
If the teacher asks — expect the hardest questions HERE
What is your novel contribution, in one line?
Teaching the model to point at hateful words using only English word-labels, and having it work on Hindi in Devanagari where no such labels exist — and we measure whether the explanation is honest, not just assume it.
How can it explain Hindi with zero Hindi word-labels?
We use a masked multi-task loss: the "highlight words" head only learns from English (which has word-labels); the "toxic/not" head learns from both. So we never train on a label we don't have — the word-skill still transfers through the shared model.
Why does the skill transfer across two different alphabets?
Because MuRIL was pre-trained on Hindi and English together, it stores meaning, not spelling. An English slur and a Hindi slur end up near each other in its internal "meaning space", so a skill learned on one fires on the other — even with zero shared letters.
Where do the English word-labels come from?
HateXplain — an English dataset where humans marked which exact words made a post hateful. That is our only source of word-level rationale.
What is a "rationale" / "faithfulness"?
Rationale = the exact words the model says are toxic (the explanation). Faithfulness = is that explanation the real reason? We test it by deleting those words: if the toxic score collapses, the explanation was real. This test needs no labels, so it works in Hindi.
What did you actually score on Hindi?
On the HASOC Hindi test set: accuracy 0.837, recall 0.819, F1 0.842, macro-F1 0.837, precision 0.866. The transformer beats a TF-IDF baseline (0.787) by 5.5 points.
What is macro-F1 and why use it here?
F1 averaged equally over both classes (toxic and not). It is honest when classes are uneven, because it won't let the big class hide poor performance on the small one.
Any honest weakness?
Yes — when we turn on the Hindi explanation objective, accuracy dips from 0.842 to 0.802 in exchange for better explanations. That accuracy-vs-explainability trade-off is exactly what we set out to measure, and reporting it is part of the contribution.
Your training settings (if pushed)?
MuRIL-base (236M). AdamW, learning rate 2e-5 for the encoder and 1e-4 for the new heads, batch of 16 (8 Hindi + 8 English), max length 128, mixed precision (fp16), decision threshold 0.5.
Is it code-mixed / Hinglish?
We measured it: about 82% of the characters are Devanagari, so it is native Hindi script, not romanized Hinglish. We analyse the small Latin part separately.
10Future Scope — Ensembling
Say it simply
"Even our best single model makes some unstable mistakes. If we run several different models and average their scores, their random mistakes point in different directions and cancel out — so the combined answer is steadier and catches more. That is ensembling, and it is our next step."
If the teacher asks — the graph / the math
What does the graph show? What is "error variance" and "K"?
Error variance = how much a model's mistakes wobble from run to run. K = how many models we average. The curve goes down: average K independent models and the wobble shrinks to about one-Kth. Two models already halve it.
Why are there two curves?
The lower (black) curve is independent models — mistakes fully cancel. The upper (grey) is correlated models — they make similar mistakes, so averaging helps less. Lesson: average diverse models (a transformer + an RNN + a bigger transformer), not copies.
Do you have proof this works?
Yes — in the Kaggle Jigsaw competition the 8th-place team averaged 4 different models; no top team used a single model.
Why haven't you done it yet? What number will you get?
It is future work — we present it with the theory and the Kaggle evidence. We will report the actual number only after we measure it; we don't quote an unmeasured result.
