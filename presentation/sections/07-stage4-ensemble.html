STAGE 4 · ENSEMBLE BY SIMPLE AVERAGING

Stage 4 — Ensemble learning by simple averaging

This is the technique we are bringing in now. The idea is short: train several different models, then average their predicted probabilities. This is called a simple average ensemble — no weights to tune, no extra model on top, just the mean of each model's P(toxic).

Why it works — the maths

The result below is standard ensemble theory, not something we measured. We follow Krogh & Vedelsby (1995). literature

Setup: K models, each with the SAME bias and error variance σ².
The simple average is  f̄ = (1/K) × Σ fi.

Case 1 — independent errors:
  bias( f̄ ) = bias( fi )  (unchanged)
  Var( f̄ ) = σ² / K  → variance shrinks, bias does not grow.

Case 2 — errors with pairwise correlation ρ:
  Var( f̄ ) = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ² / K
  As K → ∞,   Var( f̄ ) → ρσ².

⇒ the floor is set by ρ. Adding more copies of one model (high ρ) buys little; the gain comes from LOW correlation, i.e. DIVERSITY.

Ambiguity decomposition (Krogh & Vedelsby):
  ensemble error = (mean individual error) − (mean ambiguity / diversity)
  ambiguity ≥ 0 ⇒ ensemble error ≤ mean individual error.
  (For a convex loss this also follows from Jensen's inequality: loss(mean of predictions) ≤ mean of loss(predictions).)
Takeaway. Averaging cuts variance without adding bias, and the benefit scales with how uncorrelated the models' errors are. So you average diverse architectures — a transformer, a larger transformer, and an RNN — not several copies of the same model.

Evidence — what the Jigsaw competition actually did

The same Jigsaw "Unintended Bias" task we used for the English track was a Kaggle competition, so we can look at what the top teams did. literature

  • Qishen Ha's 8th-place solution was titled "8th Place Solution (4 models simple avg)" — literally four diverse models combined by simple averaging of predicted probabilities. (We have the public title and competition write-ups; we do not have the four exact architectures, so we say "four diverse models" and do not name them.)
  • The competition consensus was that a fine-tuned BERT was the best single model, but every top team ensembled diverse models — BERT-base, BERT-large, GPT-2, and LSTM-based models — by averaging or weighted-averaging (e.g. the 2nd-place "power 3.5 weighted sum"). No top team relied on a single model.
  • To handle the bias metric and the imbalance, teams also used sample weighting (up-weight toxic + identity examples; target weight × log(annotator_count + 2)) and negative down-sampling (drop 50% of negatives after epoch 1).

Multi-sample dropout — a mini-ensemble inside one model

Inoue (2019, arXiv:1905.09788) gives a cheaper, related trick that lives inside a single model. literature

  • Within ONE training iteration, draw k different dropout masks and push the input through each. The duplicated layers share weights, so there are no extra parameters.
  • Average the per-sample losses across the k masks and back-propagate that.
  • Effect: faster convergence and better generalisation at almost no extra cost — an intra-model mini-ensemble. (We describe the mechanism only; we do not quote the paper's exact error-reduction numbers, which we have not verified.)
